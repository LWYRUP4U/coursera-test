Why this guide

This is a complete, self-contained path from the Spanish alphabet to conversational A2–B1 grammar: sounds and spelling, nouns and agreement, every major verb tense and mood, sentence building, 200+ thematic vocabulary words, survival phrases, idioms, false friends, regional differences, and common mistakes to avoid — plus trackers to log your own study and listening time. Use the search bar above to jump straight to any word or phrase, or browse section by section with the sidebar.

A 12-week route through this guide. Roughly 20–40 minutes a day gets you through it.

Week 1
Alphabet, pronunciation, greetings, numbers 0–30

More Tenses & Everyday Structures

The workbook covers present, preterite, imperfect, future and conditional — here are the remaining pieces you need for real fluency: perfect tenses, the progressive, near-future plans, and gustar-type verbs.

Present Perfect (Pretérito Perfecto) — "have done"

Formed with the present tense of haber + past participle. Used for actions in a period that still includes now ("today", "this week", "ever").

Personhaber+ hablado / comido / vivido
yohehe hablado (I have spoken)
túhashas comido (you have eaten)
él/ella/Ud.haha vivido (he/she has lived)
nosotroshemoshemos hablado
vosotroshabéishabéis comido
ellos/Uds.hanhan vivido

Common irregular past participles: hacer → hecho, decir → dicho, escribir → escrito, ver → visto, poner → puesto, volver → vuelto, romper → roto, abrir → abierto, morir → muerto.

Pluperfect (Pretérito Pluscuamperfecto) — "had done"

Imperfect of haber + past participle — an action completed before another past action.

Personhaber (imperfect)Example
yohabíaYa había comido cuando llegaste. (I had already eaten when you arrived.)
túhabíashabías hablado
él/ella/Ud.habíahabía vivido
nosotroshabíamoshabíamos comido
vosotroshabíaishabíais hablado
ellos/Uds.habíanhabían vivido

Present Progressive — "is/are doing"

estar (conjugated) + gerund. The gerund is formed with -ando (-AR verbs) or -iendo (-ER/-IR verbs).

InfinitiveGerundExample
hablarhablandoEstoy hablando. (I am speaking.)
comercomiendoEstás comiendo. (You are eating.)
vivirviviendoEstá viviendo aquí. (He is living here.)
leerleyendoirregular: unstressed i → y between vowels
dormirdurmiendoirregular: stem change o→u
pedirpidiendoirregular: stem change e→i

ir a + infinitive — near future ("going to")

The everyday way to talk about plans, far more common in speech than the future tense.

StructureExample
ir (present) + a + infinitiveVoy a estudiar. (I'm going to study.)
Vamos a comer. (We're going to eat. / Let's eat!)
¿Vas a venir? (Are you going to come?)

Gustar-type verbs — flipped subject/object

Gustar literally means "to be pleasing to". The thing liked is the grammatical subject; the person is an indirect object.

PatternExampleLiteral meaning
me/te/le/nos/os/les + gusta + singular nounMe gusta el café.Coffee is pleasing to me.
me/te/le/nos/os/les + gustan + plural nounMe gustan los perros.Dogs are pleasing to me.
+ infinitive(s)Nos gusta viajar.Traveling is pleasing to us.

Other verbs that work the same way: encantar (to love/be delighted by), interesar (to interest), molestar (to bother), faltar (to be lacking), doler (to hurt/ache), parecer (to seem/appear).

Sentence Structure

Word order, forming questions, negation patterns, connectors and the key prepositions that hold a sentence together.

Basic Word Order: Subject - Verb - Object (flexible, but SVO is default)

PatternExampleTranslation
Subject + Verb + ObjectYo como pan.I eat bread.
Subject + Verb + AdjectiveElla es inteligente.She is intelligent.

Grammar Case Reference

Spanish doesn't decline nouns by case the way Latin or German does — word order, prepositions and pronouns do that job instead. This maps each grammatical role to how Spanish expresses it.

Spanish nouns don't decline by case (like Latin/German) — role is shown by WORD ORDER + PREPOSITIONS + PRONOUNS instead. This sheet maps each grammatical case/role to how Spanish expresses it.

Grammatical Roles (the Spanish equivalent of 'cases')

Case / RoleFunctionHow Spanish marks itExample
Nominative (subject)Who/what performs the actionSubject pronoun or noun, usually before verbJuan come. (Juan eats.)
Accusative (direct object)Receives the action directlyDirect object pronoun (me/te/lo/la/nos/os/los/las) or 'a' for peopleVeo a Juan. / Lo veo.
Dative (indirect object)To/for whom the action is doneIndirect object pronoun (me/te/le/nos/os/les)Le doy el libro. (I give him the book.)
Genitive (possession)Shows ownershipde + noun, or possessive adjectives (mi, tu, su...)el libro de Juan / mi libro
Vocative (direct address)Calling/addressing someone directlyName or title, often set off by commas¡Juan, ven aquí!
Ablative-like (means/manner/cause)By/with/from what meansPrepositions con, por, de, enViajo en tren. (I travel by train.)
Locative (location)Where something isPrepositions en, sobre, debajo de, cerca deEstá en la mesa. (It's on the table.)
Instrumental (tool used)With what instrumentPreposition conEscribo con un lápiz. (I write with a pencil.)

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The errors English speakers make most often — and the quick fix for each one.

Mistake 1

Confusing ser and estar

Trap: using ser for temporary states/locations
Fix: 'Estoy en casa' NOT 'Soy en casa'; 'Estoy cansado' NOT 'Soy cansado'

Mistake 2

Skipping the personal 'a'

Trap: dropping 'a' before a specific person as direct object
Fix: 'Veo a María' NOT 'Veo María'

Mistake 3

Forgetting gender agreement

Trap: mismatching article/adjective/noun gender
Fix: 'la mesa roja' NOT 'la mesa rojo'

Mistake 4

Overusing subject pronouns

Trap: saying 'yo soy' constantly when the verb ending already shows it
Fix: just 'Soy profesor' is natural; 'Yo soy profesor' can sound emphatic/unnatural

Mistake 5

Mixing up por and para

Trap: using por/para interchangeably
Fix: 'para' = purpose/destination/deadline; 'por' = cause/duration/through/exchange

Mistake 6

Literal word-for-word translation

Trap: building English syntax with Spanish words
Fix: adjectives usually go AFTER the noun: 'una casa grande' NOT 'una grande casa'

Mistake 7

Preterite vs imperfect mix-ups

Trap: using preterite for background description
Fix: use imperfect for 'was/were doing', weather, age, ongoing states: 'Era de noche' NOT 'Fue de noche'

Mistake 8

Double negatives feel wrong but are correct

Trap: avoiding 'no...nada' because it seems redundant
Fix: Spanish requires it: 'No tengo nada' (I don't have anything) is correct, not incorrect

Mistake 9

Wrong stress from a missing accent mark

Trap: dropping accents changes meaning or moves the stress
Fix: 'papa' (potato/pope) vs 'papá' (dad); 'esta' (this) vs 'está' (is)

Mistake 10

Reflexive pronoun placement

Trap: putting reflexive pronouns in the wrong spot with infinitives
Fix: both 'Me quiero levantar' and 'Quiero levantarme' are correct — pick one, don't mix

Resources & Next Steps

Free and low-cost tools to keep momentum after you finish this guide, plus a roadmap for going beyond A2.

Apps & SRS

Duolingo or Busuu for daily habit-building; Anki with a pre-made Spanish frequency deck for long-term vocabulary retention via spaced repetition.

Structured audio courses

Language Transfer's 'Spanish' course (free) teaches grammar through guided thinking, no rote memorization. Coffee Break Spanish and Notes in Spanish are strong podcast options.

Comprehensible input

Dreaming Spanish (YouTube) ranks videos by level so you can understand Spanish without subtitles from day one. Easy Spanish (street interviews) bridges textbook Spanish and real speech.

Dictionary & grammar lookup

SpanishDict for instant conjugation tables, example sentences and audio pronunciation of any word.

Reading practice

Start with graded readers or children's books, then news sites written in clear Spanish (e.g. Noticias Fáciles) before tackling native novels.

Speaking practice

italki or Tandem to book conversation partners or tutors once you're through the Present Tense and Sentence Structure sections.

Immersion

Change your phone's language to Spanish, watch one Spanish show a week with Spanish subtitles, and keep a small notebook of new words you hear.
